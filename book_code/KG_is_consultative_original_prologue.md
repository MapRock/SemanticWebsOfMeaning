This is my original prologue for the book. My objective was (and still is) to point out that AI shouldn't simply **tell** us what to do. It should work as an advisor who knows most things about most things. None of us can do that, but in the normal course of life, we're forced to step outside our expertise.


# Prologue — The Consultative Touch

For the first 20 years of my career (1979–2000), I was a programmer at software companies. At the most basic level, I had requirements that needed to be converted into code. I always had a very active role in shaping the requirements, but I thought of myself—and others thought of me—as a programmer.

But that changed in 2002 when I returned to Microsoft as an **Application Development Consultant**. What’s that?!

It sure wasn’t the same as sitting in my cube hitting the keyboard for most of the day, alone for the majority of the time. I now had multiple people to answer to, multiple processes to follow, a focus on billable hours, customer relationships and satisfaction, and work in cross-functional teams.

I had to learn about this “consulting” thing. I turned to a book that seemed to be good, and it was: [*Flawless Consulting*, by Peter Block (2000)](https://www.amazon.com/Flawless-Consulting-Guide-Getting-Expertise/dp/1394177305/ref=sr_1_1?crid=9LYK6H8061ZJ&dib=eyJ2IjoiMSJ9.-ui6783_RakRUeY54x9Z2-zHEzL6rPwqFXFqAiSSagEGRWAS2CYPSPA23P_5G5e7o4s7ylUzid0O_nGMw1lg4fukQEZWs39NPBP6y4Xc7dtYOElQ6Xa1dj-OvmtA1III1pr9Ju9gUNuZorl82VcJBVpJS0j9AQhmVzJHdF-2WCagqzEhhRpXZ2AQSk-iHC3ze0fg9cr1fYfsbHZ-n-o8rjBTSbKbea0TJ1ASQxmEvBg.hhgLyNMmUQjIAiCPIeQ5aC264vrCHWu_mAL20mZ9_Jc&dib_tag=se&keywords=Flawless+Consulting%2C+by+Peter+Block&qid=1787067669&sprefix=flawless+consulting%2C+by+peter+block%2Caps%2C242&sr=8-1).

In the preface, Block writes:

> “THIS BOOK IS FOR ANYONE who does consulting, even if you don’t call yourself a consultant. You are consulting any time you are trying to change or improve a situation but have no direct control over the implementation.”

He continues early in the book:

> “I use the word consulting here in a very general sense, to cover many functions. Anyone doing ‘staff work’ is consulting… Most people in staff roles in organizations are really consultants, even if they don’t officially call themselves ‘consultants.’”

Those words were a revelation. What I had thought was a brand-new professional identity suddenly felt like something far more fundamental and familiar—something I had already been doing for years in every advisory role, every staff position, and every time I tried to bring expertise or influence positive change without having direct authority. It showed me that I had always been a consultant, and I always will be. In fact, we’re all consultants, with a diversity of knowledge forged from unique combinations of experiences.

Similarly, most software developed in enterprises before ChatGPT burst onto the mainstream scene in November 2022 was only required to automate well-defined tasks and processes. There was no expectation for the software to do anything other than what it was programmed to do. People wished it could.

Although software still can’t—and arguably shouldn’t—default to swimming outside of its lane, we now have the ability to close the gap between our human intelligence and the software. We have the means to encode our knowledge and expertise into a reusable, interpretable format.

Knowledge graphs move our data systems closer to consulting capability. Until AI, “active” consulting knowledge existed only in the heads of people. By “active,” I mean as opposed to knowledge in books that must be mined—read—which is what LLMs do.

For example, we could walk into a hardware store and know we’re looking for a **1/2"-13 × 2" Grade 8 hex cap screw**. We could ask someone working there where it is, in the mode of a simple search.

Or we could ask whether there is something we could inexpensively hack together to soundproof an office window. That probably requires more experience and connected knowledge spanning disparate disciplines and components.

LLMs may not truly understand a topic to an extent as immersive as a human consultant, but they can at least reflect the value of a human consultant to a significant degree. Understanding requires connecting an unfragmented web of rabbit holes, modes of presentation, and ways of processing data.

Consulting provides a paradigm shift in value beyond research. That is readily evident in the evolution of Google—from algorithmic search to search augmented by Gemini, adding a consultative touch. That is also what knowledge graphs can add on top of traditional enterprise software backed by SQL databases such as Oracle and SQL Server.

**Search retrieves, software executes, and a consultant interprets. A knowledge graph is one way to move enterprise systems closer to interpretation while maintaining governance.**
