# Appendix B — The Quartz Cycle That Flipped the Switch for Me

One of the first geological ideas that substantially changed how I thought about rocks was something a friend called the quartz cycle. I am using that phrase here as the name he gave the idea and as a conceptual teaching device, not as a claim that geology contains one canonical closed “quartz cycle.”

In geological terms, what he was really showing me was something like a quartz-centered path through the much broader and conventional **rock cycle**.

Before that conversation, my picture of geology was largely a collection of simple relationships. Volcanoes produced lava. Coral produced sand. Rocks had names, and particular things produced particular kinds of rocks.

The quartz-cycle story gave me something much more powerful: history.

Start with granite. Granite is a crystalline igneous rock commonly composed largely of quartz and feldspar with mica and other minerals. When granite is exposed at the surface, physical and chemical weathering begin dismantling it. Feldspars can chemically weather into clay minerals and mica and other minerals can break down, while quartz is comparatively resistant. Individual quartz grains can therefore survive after much of the surrounding rock has changed.

Water, gravity, glaciers, wind, and other processes can transport those grains. In the simple version that first made sense to me, I imagined quartz released from granite moving downhill through drainages and rivers until it accumulated on plains, beaches, or in the ocean.

With continued deposition, younger sediment buries the older material. Compaction and cementation can turn quartz-rich sand into sandstone. The cement need not itself always be silica; silica, calcite, iron oxides, and other materials can bind sedimentary grains depending on the geological setting.

Bury sandstone more deeply and subject it to sufficient heat and pressure, and its quartz grains can recrystallize and interlock to form quartzite.

But the interesting part is that the real story immediately begins to branch.

One branch goes upward. Sandstone, quartzite, granite, or other rocks can be uplifted and exposed by tectonics and erosion. Once exposed, weathering begins again. Quartz can therefore return to sediment without the rock ever entering a subduction zone, melting, or completing the large loop shown in the figure.

Another branch goes deeper into metamorphism. Quartzite itself does not simply and inevitably “turn into gneiss” as the next step. But quartz-bearing rocks subjected to high-grade metamorphism can participate in more complicated metamorphic histories, and rocks with appropriate mixtures of quartz, feldspar, mica, and other minerals can develop gneissic textures and compositions. Granitic rocks themselves can also become gneiss under intense metamorphism and deformation.

Still another path leads toward partial melting. Deep burial and heating can eventually cause some crustal rocks to begin melting, producing migmatitic conditions and magma. If suitable magma later cools slowly at depth, it can crystallize into granite or another intrusive igneous rock.

Subduction is one mechanism capable of carrying material deep into the Earth, but it is not the only tectonic route through the story. Continental collision and crustal thickening can also deeply bury, heat, deform, and metamorphose rocks. Uplift works in the other direction, carrying once-deep rocks back toward the surface where erosion exposes them and starts shallower parts of the cycle again.

So the real picture is not really:

**A → B → C → D → A**

It is closer to a graph:

**weathering → transport → deposition → burial → metamorphism → deformation → uplift → erosion → partial melting → crystallization**

with many possible routes among them.

Nor should “the quartz cycle” imply that one immortal quartz crystal (zircon is closer to “immortal”) necessarily completes the entire journey. At sufficiently high temperatures minerals react, recrystallize, and may melt. It is better understood as the recycling of quartz and quartz-bearing crustal material through geological processes.

The scientific qualifications came later. What mattered initially was the conceptual change. A piece of granite was no longer merely granite. Its quartz might become sand. The sand might become sandstone. The sandstone might become quartzite. That rock might later be uplifted and eroded again, pushed deeper into another metamorphic history, or eventually participate in melting and igneous formation.

## The Rock had a Past and Possible Futures

That simple realization did more for my understanding of geology than memorizing a large number of specimens could have done. It gave me a causal-process model into which new observations could be connected. When I pick up an unfamiliar rock now, I am not simply comparing its appearance with samples I have previously been told the names of. I am trying—imperfectly—to place it somewhere in a history of materials, processes, environments, and transformations.

That is why the quartz cycle belongs in a blog about Expert Reasoning Graphs. It is an example of how a relatively small piece of explanatory knowledge can reorganize many otherwise isolated facts into a traversable model.
