# Appendix D – The Knowledge Worker as the Next Layer of the Solution Library

One reason I am interested in Expert Reasoning Graphs is that they fit into a much longer story I have been writing about for years: **where solutions come from and how they survive long enough for someone else to build upon them.**

The story begins long before human intelligence.

Evolution spent hundreds of millions of years stumbling upon workable solutions to problems imposed by the environment. Eyes, wings, camouflage, immune systems, roots, shells, symbiosis, migration, pack behavior and countless other adaptations are solutions that survived because they worked well enough under particular conditions.

Nature therefore became humanity's first enormous library of working examples.

Humans did not have to reinvent everything from first principles. We could observe something that already worked, recognize an analogous problem of our own, abstract part of the solution, and modify it. A bird's wing does not give us an airplane blueprint, but it provides clues about lift and controlled movement through air. Natural systems similarly inspired materials, structures, medicines, organizations, strategies and countless other human inventions.

Over thousands of years, however, something important changed.

Human beings began adding their own successful solutions back into the library.

A person no longer had to look only to nature. They could learn from tools, stories, buildings, governments, mathematics, scientific theories, recipes, machines, software, procedures and the experiences of other people. Each generation inherited an increasingly enormous body of solutions produced by previous generations. Those solutions could themselves become the analogies from which still newer solutions were constructed.

The library became cumulative.

The Expert Reasoning Graph therefore feels less to me like an isolated graph idea than another step in that progression.

Evolution retains what worked. Culture retains some of what humans learned. Books, software, procedures and other encoded forms make portions of culture durable.

Knowledge workers then spend their careers adapting that inherited knowledge to circumstances nobody anticipated exactly.

**The Expert Reasoning Graph is an attempt to keep more of what happens in that last step from disappearing.**

## The Knowledge Worker Adds Another Layer

But there is another layer that does not receive nearly as much attention.

Consider a person who has spent twenty or thirty years doing a particular kind of work.

They inherited the cultural library available when they entered the profession: books, training, formal education, documentation, procedures, software, stories from coworkers, and established ways of solving known problems.

Then reality happened.

They encountered thousands of particular situations that nobody who wrote those materials could have anticipated exactly.

A database performance specialist sees strange combinations of workloads, hardware, software versions, query patterns, configuration choices and organizational constraints. A geologist encounters unusual rocks in particular landscapes. A security investigator sees attack paths that combine familiar techniques in unfamiliar ways. A nurse, mechanic, engineer, salesperson, farmer or analyst accumulates a similarly unique history.

Over time, the knowledge worker modifies what was inherited.

They discover that a documented procedure works except under one peculiar condition. They notice that a seemingly unimportant clue frequently precedes a particular failure. They learn which measurement to examine first, which warning can usually be ignored, which two symptoms appearing together should make them suspicious, and when a supposedly unrelated event is probably important.

Sometimes they deliberately reason their way to those discoveries.

Sometimes another person teaches them.

Sometimes they borrow an idea from another domain.

And sometimes they simply stumble onto something that works, recognize its value, and remember it.

Two people holding the same job for twenty years therefore do not necessarily end up possessing the same knowledge. Their experience consists of different cases, different failures, different coworkers, different mistakes, different analogies and different discoveries.

Their accumulated expertise becomes another layer of the solution library.

## Much of That Layer Never Makes It Back Into Culture

Some of these discoveries eventually escape the individual's head.

Someone writes a book. A programmer documents an obscure behavior. A support engineer adds an article to a knowledge base. A postmortem records why a system failed. A new procedure incorporates a lesson learned from an unusual incident.

When that happens, individual experience becomes part of the larger cultural library and can be inherited by people who never experienced the original problem.

But much of it never makes that transition.

- The problem gets solved and everyone moves on.
- The support ticket is closed.
- The database begins running normally again.
- The customer is retained.
- The machine is repaired.
- The strange rock is identified.

The reasoning that produced the answer remains with the person who solved the problem.

This is what I referred to years ago as **tribal knowledge**: knowledge that can become extremely rich through tens of thousands of hours working within an enterprise, but which may remain almost entirely inside the heads of the people doing the work.

Good documentation does not completely solve this problem. Documentation generally records what the system is, how it is supposed to work, and how known tasks should be performed. Even excellent documentation cannot anticipate every unusual combination of circumstances that experienced people will encounter.

The most interesting expertise often begins where that documentation ends.

## From the Products of Expertise to the Reasoning Behind Them

I previously described a progression something like:

**evolution (genes) → culture (memes) → encoded forms**

Encoded forms are the durable products of human reasoning: plans, stories, procedures, strategies, models, diagrams, checklists, recipes and other artifacts that can survive the mind that produced them.

The Expert Reasoning Graph suggests another step. A procedure records a solution.

An ERG attempts to preserve enough of the **reasoning that generated or adapted the solution** that the knowledge can be useful when the next case is not identical.

That means capturing things such as:

- What did the expert notice?
- What context caused that observation to matter?
- What possibilities came to mind?
- What mechanisms could explain what was happening?
- What evidence supported or contradicted each possibility?
- What did the expert expect to observe if a hypothesis was correct?
- What did they decide to examine next?
- What exception caused them to abandon the normal procedure?
- What changed their mind?
- What did they learn from the case that might transfer to another one?

That is the knowledge-worker layer I am trying to reach with an Expert Reasoning Graph.

## The Complete Chain

Seen this way, there is a long chain of accumulated problem solving:

| Layer | Source of solutions | How solutions are retained | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Evolution** | Variation and selection operating across enormous spans of time | Genes, anatomy and behavior | Wings, camouflage, vision, symbiosis |
| **Human observation and analogy** | Recognition that a natural solution might apply to a human problem | Individual and shared learning | Flight inspired partly by observing birds |
| **Culture** | Solutions discovered, adapted and recombined by generations of humans | Language, teaching, books, tools, institutions and technology | Engineering, medicine, mathematics, cooking |
| **Encoded knowledge** | Deliberate reasoning turns experience into reusable artifacts | Plans, models, procedures, recipes, checklists, software and documentation | A troubleshooting guide or design pattern |
| **Knowledge-worker experience** | Repeated exposure to particular cases, exceptions and changing circumstances | Primarily human memory and informal sharing | “When these two strange symptoms occur together, I check this first.” |
| **Expert Reasoning Graph** | Deliberate elicitation of transferable expert reasoning | Traversable observations, context, mechanisms, hypotheses, evidence, investigations and revisions | Why an expert suspects an unusual cause and what they would examine next |

The first layers have been accumulating for an extraordinarily long time.

The weak link is near the bottom. Humanity is very good at preserving finished products. We preserve recipes, designs, formulas, procedures, theories and software. Enterprises preserve enormous quantities of transactions and measurements.

We are much worse at preserving **how a particular experienced person navigated from an unfamiliar situation to a solution when no existing recipe quite fit.**

That knowledge is constantly being created and constantly disappearing.

## Why LLMs Change the Economics

This also helps explain why I think Expert Reasoning Graphs are substantially more feasible now.

The LLM already contains a large portion of the cultural layer.

Unless the occupation is extraordinarily exotic, new, or proprietary, a modern LLM probably knows enough about the general domain to understand much of what an expert is talking about. The database expert does not need to explain what an index is. The geologist does not need to begin by defining erosion. The lawyer does not need to explain what precedent means.

The conversation can begin much farther into the weeds.

The expert can spend the limited time describing what is **different about their experience**: the unusual cases, local conditions, undocumented behaviors, exceptions, surprising connections and hard-earned judgment.

The LLM can then help compare those statements with the broader body of existing knowledge, ask questions, expose assumptions, suggest terminology, distinguish observations from interpretations, identify possible mechanisms, and organize the result into a candidate graph.

The expert still has to decide whether it is right.

That last point is crucial. The objective is not to replace the unique knowledge-worker layer with whatever an LLM already knows. If we did that, we would merely reproduce the existing cultural library and lose precisely the information we are trying to capture.

The objective is to use that enormous existing library as scaffolding so that the **new knowledge produced by this particular person's experience** can be identified, articulated and preserved.
